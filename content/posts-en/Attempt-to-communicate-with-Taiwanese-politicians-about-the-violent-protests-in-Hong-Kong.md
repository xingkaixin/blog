## I Wrote an Email to Taipei City Councilor Chiu Wei-chieh

Hi Noki,
This email is for Froggy (呱吉). I checked Froggy's Facebook and Instagram and couldn't find his personal email address, so I am sending this to the business inquiries address instead. Please excuse my poor command of Traditional Chinese—I typed the entire email in Simplified Chinese and converted it to Traditional Chinese via Google Translate.

Hi Froggy,
I am from Shanghai and a viewer of the NSFW (上班不要看) channel, as well as many other Taiwanese YouTube channels such as 欸你這週要幹嘛, Men's Game 玩物誌, 旅行YJ, 飽妮, Lo-Fi House, 台客劇場 TKstory, and 啾啾鞋.
I visited Taiwan for the first time in late October 2018 for a 15-day trip and loved it. Of course, due to policy changes, I can no longer visit Taiwan as an independent traveler.

In your June 19, 2019 livestream, *【呱吉直播】政治電台EP3：台北與香港的終局之戰*, and your June 26, 2019 video, *【呱吉】我們是暴民嗎？香港反送中運動。*, at that point in time, from what I could see, the Hong Kong protests looked like a completely normal demonstration to voice demands, with much of the focus on questioning excessive policing by the Hong Kong police. Since then, however, the protests have grown increasingly strange.

On August 12, protesters entered Hong Kong International Airport, forcing all flights to be grounded, and an assault took place during the incident (Wikipedia link: https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/付国豪遇袭事件).

After that, videos of violent acts by people dressed in black kept surfacing. Entering October, the protests grew more intense, the MTR was vandalized, and on October 4, after the Hong Kong government announced the anti-mask law, large numbers of protesters blocked roads, set fires, and vandalized property, leading the entire MTR network to suspend service.

Some videos of violent acts

I'm not sure how you view the violence occurring during the Hong Kong protests now, or whether you might make a video or do a livestream to talk about it.

## Some Thoughts

Regarding the Hong Kong protests, in early June I thought it was a normal demonstration with nothing wrong with it, nor did it break Hong Kong's laws. Even though I didn't see an issue with the extradition bill itself, if people in Hong Kong felt there was a problem and came out to oppose it, I wouldn't consider that illegitimate. However, the situation kept escalating, with more and more violent acts emerging—and that has clearly crossed the line. Among the YouTube channels I follow, some people have shouted "Add oil, Hong Kong" (香港加油). Considering the phrase itself is harmless—even if their stance is biased—these YouTubers aren't politicians after all. That's why I chose to reach out and try to have a conversation with a newly elected Taipei City Councilor instead.
