We are surrounded by rules. Some we know well, so we follow them; others we are unaware of, so we end up breaking them from time to time before getting fined or called out.

In my view, these rules exist so that our society can continue to run in an orderly way.

Sometime last year, my parents' residential compound introduced a one-way traffic system. The main road from the entrance to the exit became a one-way lane, with road markings painted on the ground and signs put up at the entrance. Once I learned about this, every time I drove to my parents' place, I dutifully took the long detour around. Following this rule meant I didn't have to worry about meeting an oncoming car and being forced to reverse on a narrow, crowded neighborhood road at the risk of scraping something.

Where some people follow the rules, others break them. Over this period, it kept happening from time to time: some were outside vehicles unfamiliar with the layout of the compound, others probably didn't know about the rule yet, and still others broke it again and again simply for their own convenience.

This afternoon, on my way to my parents' place, I ran into an older driver who had been blocked by another driver going the right way. He acted as if he were the victim, and even with more than a dozen cars backed up behind, he refused to give way at all. When the compound's property security guards were called in and clearly explained the rule and tried to persuade him, he would only back up slightly—just enough to leave a narrow gap for others to squeeze past him. All the while, he kept making unreasonable excuses and hurling insults.

Fed up with rule-breakers like this, I decided to call the police, hoping they could clarify how traffic disputes inside a residential compound should be handled. While I was on the phone, the older man kept muttering nonstop and even mocked me for supposedly not knowing how to make a police report, when I was just giving the officer directions on how to get there. Judging by their uniforms, the officers who arrived were auxiliary police. After learning what had happened, they took a very clear stance and told the older man to back up and clear the road so everyone could drive straight through. When he kept stalling shamelessly, they asked to record his vehicle registration and driver's license—and at that point he finally backed down and reversed out of the way. The whole ordeal took an hour. Over a dozen cars were stuck at the start; after criticizing him to no avail, most chose to reverse and take another route. In the end, only two cars stayed and saw it through.

Looking at how it all turned out, my one small regret is this: when the older man first backed up a bit, I actually could have driven through already, but when I asked him whether he thought he had done anything wrong at all, he shamelessly claimed he hadn't made a single mistake and that all of us "terrible people" were just getting in his way. I told the auxiliary officers I hoped he could recognize his mistake and apologize, though in the end that never happened.

On the bright side, next time I run into someone driving the wrong way and refusing to yield, I know that calling the police actually works.
