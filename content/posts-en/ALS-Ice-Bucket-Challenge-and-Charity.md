I saw LBJ getting a bucket of ice water dumped on him on a yacht on Instagram today. Apparently he was accepting KD's challenge, and before doing it he named three other people to take it on next, including Obama. It looked pretty cool, though I was out at the time and only gave it a quick glance.

When I got home, I looked it up. Turns out ALS stands for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, a disease famously associated with the physicist Stephen Hawking. Recently, the US launched a campaign called the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge to raise public awareness of ALS and support for patients. The rules are simple: a challenger nominates three people, and anyone who declines has to donate $100. Taking the challenge means filming a video of yourself getting drenched from head to toe with a bucket of ice water.

At 5 p.m., Xiaomi's Lei Jun posted a [Weibo](http://weibo.com/1749127163/BiInvk0GE?mod=weibotime&type=comment) accepting the challenge, and followed up with a post explaining what the campaign is about. By 7 p.m., OnePlus's Pete Lau posted his own [challenge video on Weibo](http://weibo.com/1110411735/BiJqZFebZ?mod=weibotime), nominating Zhou Hongyi of Qihoo 360, Luo Yonghao of Smartisan, and Liu Jiangfeng of Huawei Honor. Looks like all the big names in the Chinese smartphone scene are going to be doing this over the next few days.

I grabbed a few challenge videos from YouTube and re-uploaded them so you can check them out [here](http://www.tudou.com/listplay/Q4P5lEdojMI.html).

That's enough about the Ice Bucket Challenge itself. What I really want to talk about today is charity.

In China, there is a philanthropist named Chen Guangbiao, often called "Brother Biao." His approach to charity is to go to disaster zones and hand out cash. Not long ago, he even went to the US and handed out money to homeless people on the streets of New York. Over the years, this high-profile style of philanthropy has brought him plenty of honors and a huge boost in fame. Do I think this kind of charity is right? I completely disagree with it. First, take his trip to the US to hand out cash to the homeless. Anyone living in China has seen homeless people too—they aren't all elderly, sick, or disabled; plenty are young and able-bodied, for all sorts of reasons. When it comes to homeless people in the US, you have to consider whether someone might be a drug addict. Handing cash to an addict only hurts them. How is that charity? That's why I oppose high-profile philanthropy. Charity should be done quietly and modestly. High-profile charity is just spending money to put on a show—it's despicable.

Now look at the ALS Ice Bucket Challenge. For the big shots you see on stage right now, a mere $100 is pocket change. You can bet that while taking the challenge, they're also donating a sizable sum. More importantly, by participating in person, they amplify the reach and bring more and more people's attention to the cause. Ultimately, getting patients the care and attention they deserve from society is the real goal. At the same time, setting the bar at $100 lets ordinary people take part too, and gives participants a real sense of pride—look, I'm doing the exact same thing as these big names.

Letting charity return to its true purpose is what people actually want to see. Over the past few years, China has been hit by plenty of natural disasters, accompanied by plenty of fundraising drives. Every time donations come up, people inevitably climb onto a moral high horse and question how much companies are donating. Honestly, these companies pay their taxes like they're supposed to. When something happens, what right does the public have to emotionally blackmail them into donating? It's shameless. And what role is the government playing in all this? What are all those taxes being collected for?

On a slight tangent, I personally really dislike donating money. Psychology says a lot of our adult tendencies come from childhood wounds. I remember there was a donation drive back in high school. As students, we obviously had to ask our parents for the money. Most people gave 1 yuan; I gave 10. That should have been the end of it, except a few days later the classmate collecting the money told me that the extra 9 yuan I donated had been put toward class dues. What the hell?! Kids in my day didn't have access to as much information or know as much as kids today, so I just foolishly let it slide, but I felt completely cheated. Ever since that incident, I've had a strong aversion to donating.
