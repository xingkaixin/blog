In 2023, right when GPT-4 laid the capability gap bare for everyone to see, I made a sweeping call on Chinese LLMs: they were done for, about to be left in the dust.

The evidence at the time was neat and tidy. Top Chinese models trailed by a wide margin, compute was constrained, and both products and developer ecosystems were playing catch-up. From that vantage point, saying "Chinese LLMs won't catch up in the near term" wasn't unreasonable.

What I actually said was "done for." Those words had no deadline, and they left no room for things to turn.

Starting in 2025, Chinese open-source models began going toe-to-toe with closed-source ones. They aren't ahead on every front, but the gap is no longer so wide that you can't see a path to catching up. That blunt verdict I delivered back then no longer holds today.

Only later did I realize what that statement was really missing: a deadline.

## Today's Evidence Can't Answer "Forever"

"Given the current gap, it will be hard to catch up in the next year or two" is a testable prediction. It has a starting point and a time horizon, and it can be updated as reality shifts.

"Chinese LLMs have no chance" turns a present state into a permanent trait. The sentence only drops a few words, yet the claim it carries changes completely.

We take this shortcut all the time. Watch a company ship a string of weak products, and we jump from "they're behind right now" to "this company is finished." See one technical approach pull ahead for a season, and we casually declare "this is how things will work from now on." The first half describes the present; the second half has already decided the world three years out.

Judgments like that sound forceful because they strip out the conditions. And precisely because they strip out the conditions, when reality turns a corner, we can't even tell where we went wrong: did we misread the evidence, botch the reasoning, or let our forecast's time horizon quietly expire?

That's where my 2023 "done for" call went off the rails. The information back then supported "hard to catch up in the short term," not "no chance in the long run." I stated the first conclusion as if it were the second.

## Over Longer Horizons, Feedback Decides the Outcome

In the short term, inertia usually wins. Existing model gaps, compute reserves, talent density, and product market share don't vanish in a few months. Extrapolating the current trend to predict the next quarter often works well enough.

Stretch the clock to three years, and feedback beyond inertia starts reshaping the outcome.

Falling behind redirects capital. Restrictions force new technical paths. Talent moves, companies reorganize, and failing products swap out leadership and start over. Other players in the race see the same trend line and act to bend it.

So to forecast three months out, look closely at the current gap. To forecast three years out, you also have to judge how the system will respond to that gap. Long-term predictions have to price in the reactions a trend triggers, and that step is much harder.

This is why "Google is behind right now" and "Google is in permanent decline" are not the same proposition. To back up the second claim, listing the mindshare it's losing today isn't enough. You also have to show that its research talent, compute, cash flow, and distribution channels can't be turned into the next adjustment.

What long-term forecasts miss most often is this capacity to adapt. We draw trends as straight lines, while everyone else in the system refuses to stand still and let the line pass through them.

## "Reasonable at the Time" Is Not a Free Pass

An outcome that differs from what you expected doesn't prove your judgment process was bad. An event with a 70% chance of happening can still fail to happen—that's just the 30% showing up. Judging a forecast purely by a single outcome makes it easy to mistake luck for skill, or treat an unlikely event as inevitable in hindsight.

Still, "it was reasonable at the time" can't excuse every miss.

If a judgment has no time horizon, no rough probability, and no mention of what new facts would make you change your mind, it's barely testable. When it pans out, you claim foresight; when it doesn't, you retreat to "I was just going on what we knew then." Phrasing like that can never lose, which means it carries almost no information.

Looking back, my 2023 call clearly overstepped. Later events only prompted me to re-examine it; the flaw was already there the moment I said it. It dressed up a time-bound probability call as a permanent verdict.

A postmortem has to carry forward to the next call: faced with the same kind of evidence again, can I draw a cleaner boundary around my judgment?

## Next Time I Make a Call, I'll Add Three Lines

When I write a forecast now, I force myself to fill in three things: how far out the judgment reaches, what odds I'm willing to put on it, and what facts would make me reverse course.

A testable statement looks roughly like this:

> As of today, I think A is more likely to happen over the next 12 months, at roughly 70% probability, based on X and Y. If B shows up, I will revise this down; if C shows up, I will revise it up.

![A testable judgment needs a deadline, a probability, and reversal conditions](/posts/images/judgment-needs-a-deadline/judgment-needs-a-deadline-03.png)

That phrasing doesn't punch as hard as "definitely" or "forever," but it sits much closer to what we actually know. Spelling out the conditions makes a judgment testable. The clearer those conditions are, the easier it is for others to spot what you missed—and the harder it is for your future self to quietly move the goalposts.

Standing back in 2023, here is what I wish I had said instead: the odds of Chinese LLMs catching up to the leading closed-source models in the next year or two are low; if open-source efforts noticeably narrow the capability gap, or find much more efficient training and inference methods under tight compute constraints, I will update my view.

That version still makes a real bet, while leaving a door open for reality.

Next time you're about to say "that company is finished" or "that approach is dead," put a deadline on it first—then write down what would make you change your mind.

**A long-term forecast without a deadline or reversal conditions usually just records today's mood.**
