HBO's _The Last of Us_ is adapted from the 2013 action video game of the same name developed by Naughty Dog, with the game's creative director and writer Neil Druckmann serving as showrunner alongside writer-director Craig Mazin (_Chernobyl_). The series stays close to the original game's storyline, keeping many plot points and key scenes intact—even lifting dialogue and scene transitions verbatim. For anyone familiar with the game, that comes as no surprise.

For viewers who know _The Last of Us_ well and care deeply about it, the HBO series is at its best and most compelling when it departs from the source material. Some of those departures, however, also introduce weaknesses. Even so, _The Last of Us_ is a worthwhile exploration of the original narrative that succeeds in carving out an identity of its own.

## Opening

The story is set in a post-apocalyptic world devastated by a parasitic fungus that turns humans into zombie-like monsters. Joel Miller is a survivor tasked with smuggling a teenage girl, Ellie, to a research facility run by a rebel group. Having lost his own child, Joel is reluctant to form any attachment to Ellie, yet through repeated trials and hardships, the two eventually build a deep emotional bond. HBO's _The Last of Us_ covers the events of _The Last of Us Part I_, including the story from its DLC.

Compared to the video game, Joel in the series is more emotionally complex—and ultimately more emotionally open. That is fortunate, since we spend less time with him over the course of the show than we do in the game. In the back half of the season, we see his violent side, yet more time is devoted to exploring his psychology and humanity. Across these nine episodes, his characterization feels more empathetic.

Naturally, the adaptation's greatest strength comes from its television format and its ability to step away from Joel and Ellie's story. _The Last of Us_ jumps freely across time and history, drawing clearer parallels between past and present events and character motivations. The show makes generous use of flashbacks—even dedicating entire episodes to characters outside the central pair—and it works remarkably well.

For anyone familiar with the timeline of both _The Last of Us_ games, these additions are fascinating and help make the television version's dynamics even more engaging.

Compared to the original game, the series also explores the origins, spread, and behavior of the fungal outbreak in greater detail, especially in the cold opens of the first two episodes—one of which takes place in Jakarta and is performed entirely in Indonesian. Those scenes are superb, and you can feel Mazin's _Chernobyl_ influence as the show settles into a slow-building dread around an impending disaster the audience already knows is coming.

## Story

HBO's _The Last of Us_ also breaks away from the original game's sequence of events, reimagining several subplots, mostly in the middle episodes written solely by Craig Mazin. These stories center on other survivor groups—Bill and Frank, as well as Henry and Sam—though the two arcs differ sharply in tone, execution, and lasting impact.

In the TV adaptation, the relationship between survivalist Bill and his partner Frank is dramatically expanded, easily standing out as one of the most memorable highlights of the entire season. Offerman is terrific in a role that demands both intensity and vulnerability, while Bartlett brings a commanding, charismatic presence. The episode centered on them elevates the entire adaptation.

The story of brothers Henry and Sam also receives an interesting reinterpretation: the younger Sam is now deaf, adding a new dimension and context to their relationship. However, their arc is intertwined with that of Kathleen, the leader of a civilian militia that seized control of Kansas City by overthrowing the government military.

Although Lynskey's performance is compelling—keeping the understated, gentle demeanor of her earlier roles to mask a ruthless streak—the storyline involving her, Henry, and Sam pulls _The Last of Us_ into more familiar zombie-fiction territory and ends up feeling more contrived than the rest of the show. It seems designed primarily to set up one of the series' few large-scale action set pieces, rather than contributing meaningfully to the already established and far more resonant dramatic conclusion of Henry and Sam's story.

On the other hand, the episodes exploring Ellie's past relationship with her close friend Riley mirror the DLC very closely. Likewise, watching Ellie set out alone in the bitter winter and encounter the Silver Lake community is just as gripping as it was in the game. These episodes give Bella Ramsey room to truly showcase her talent, and she delivers.

## Shortcomings

Because the final episodes of Season 1 adhere so closely to the source material, the dramatic finale of _The Last of Us_ does not hit quite as hard as it should. That may partly be a personal reaction from knowing the game so well, yet looking at the pacing of the HBO season as a whole, the show could have spent more time developing the bond between Joel and Ellie. Intimate moments between them are certainly there, but given the nine-episode run and the (worthwhile) decision to tell stories beyond Joel and Ellie, the growth of their eventual unbreakable bond does not feel quite as earned. Quiet moments between the two are sprinkled throughout the season, including several camping scenes where the passage of time and the need for food, rest, and sleep are felt more acutely than in the game—a welcome touch. The show also relies on Ellie's infamous joke book, _No Pun Intended_, and Joel's reactions to it as a regular, obvious barometer of where their relationship stands, yet another episode focused more tightly on their character drama would not have hurt.

While the adaptation uses Ellie's relationships with Riley and eventually Maria to examine the lives of girls and women in this largely patriarchal post-apocalyptic world, its handling of Joel's partner and companion Tess is somewhat disappointing. Torv gives a strong dramatic performance, yet compared to the game's version of Tess, HBO's Tess rarely gets the chance to show her dynamic, commanding side—one that even rivals Joel in toughness. Instead, the series seems to emphasize how age leaves her vulnerable in a harsh world, making her story feel less consequential and a bit of a letdown.
Even so, there are plenty of fresh touches that enrich the stories and characterization of these already well-formed characters, making this version of _The Last of Us_ feel grounded, distinct, and absorbing—a show about finding meaning in life when all hope seems lost. Its portrayal of Joel, Ellie, and the world they inhabit is outstanding, and the ways it diverges from the source material largely succeed in strengthening its storytelling.

Here is hoping the rest of Joel and Ellie's story receives the same treatment.
