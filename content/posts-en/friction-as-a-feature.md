A couple of days ago, I was freeing up disk space on my Mac and peeked inside `~/workspace/toy/`.

Over twenty projects were sitting there in neat little folders: `x-thread-archiver`, `cf-d1-notion-sync`, `pdf-invoice-renamer`, `vcard-to-sqlite`...

Click into any of them and the commit history is spotless, all generated by Claude Code or Cursor. Every unit test passes. Some even have GitHub Actions workflows and a Dockerfile, all dressed up like real software.

Yet I couldn't even remember what half of them were originally for.

## Built in Five Minutes, Dead on the First Real Invoice

Take `pdf-invoice-renamer`. It started one day when I was filing expense reports and got annoyed at renaming invoice PDFs one by one.

Two years ago, I would have sighed, glanced at each file, and renamed them by hand. I knew that rolling my own tool meant installing Poppler, wrangling Python's `pdfplumber`, and handling weird layouts from different tax-control systems—an entire evening gone with no guarantee it would even work.

Last month, though, I acted on impulse and typed a one-line prompt straight into the terminal.

Claude Code moved fast. In under five minutes, it spun up a Rust CLI with multithreaded extraction. The moment I ran it, the unit tests passed and cleanly formatted filenames scrolled down my terminal. I even had a brief burst of misplaced confidence that I could package it up and launch it on Product Hunt that same night.

The next day, I ran it against real invoices, and everything fell apart. An electronic invoice with a dual-layer text stream broke the regex immediately, dumping a full screen of garbled UTF-8 decoding errors into my terminal.

If I had written that code line by line myself, I probably would have traced the bug and fixed the parser. Instead, staring at two hundred lines of Rust an agent had spat out in one breath, my first thought was: whatever, I already filed this month's expenses—leave it for now.

That project has been rotting on my local disk ever since, never opened again.

## Annoying Engineering Friction Used to Be a Sanity Brake

The agonizing friction we used to feel while writing code actually saved me more times than I can count.

Just running `pnpm create`, configuring `tsconfig.json`, fixing path aliases in `vite.config.ts`, and fighting ESLint errors was enough to wear down most spur-of-the-moment impulses. Back then, that setup hell felt unbearable. In reality, it worked like a heavy physical brake pad—kicking you awake right before you blew an entire weekend on a fake problem you only cared about for five minutes.

When implementation had a real cost, you subconsciously weighed the trade-off: do I actually need to build this? If the answer was no, most bad ideas died before you even finished installing dependencies.

Now, LLMs have pulled those brake pads right off.

## The Cheaper Production Gets, the More Expensive Verification and Maintenance Become

Once the marginal cost of building approaches zero, any half-baked thought that crosses your mind can turn into a full-stack project in the time it takes to sip your water.

But the conservation law of systems engineering hasn't gone anywhere: the keystrokes you save turn into terminal tabs you never close, dozens of half-finished feature branches, and piles of black-box code you can't even bring yourself to read a second time.

When writing code feels too effortless, it creates the illusion that you're in total control. You think you're building productivity tools, when really you're just dumping disposable digital trash onto your disk at high concurrency.

The moment real-world edge cases hit, nobody wants to maintain an implementation they never bothered to think through in the first place.

## Putting a 48-Hour Cooling-Off Period on Impulse

Last week, I deleted the entire `~/workspace/toy/` directory.

I also set a hard rule for myself: the next time an idea pops up to "quickly whip up a little tool," no matter how much my fingers itch, I am not allowed to open the terminal right away.

Instead, I write the requirement on a sticky note, slap it onto the bezel of my monitor, and let it sit there for two full days.

If, after forty-eight hours, the invoices still need renaming, the workflow is still choking me, and the tool is genuinely indispensable—then I can open my laptop and let an agent build it.

![Eight sticky notes waiting forty-eight hours on a monitor bezel, with none surviving](/posts/images/friction-as-a-feature/friction-as-a-feature-02.png)

Of the seven or eight ideas I've stuck to that monitor bezel so far, not a single one has survived forty-eight hours.

**That irritating friction you feel when writing code was never a productivity bottleneck; it was the last brake pedal keeping you from manufacturing more digital junk.**
