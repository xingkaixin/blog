As a Shanghai native who isn't exclusionary and has plenty of non-Shanghai friends, I'd like to answer this question.

For examples 1 and 3, Shanghai public transit jobs are restricted to local hukou holders, and younger people rarely go into that line of work anymore. The older drivers and attendants don't necessarily speak Mandarin well.
For example 2, convenience store clerks in Shanghai used to include a large number of "4050" women (middle-aged workers in their 40s and 50s), so it's the same reason.

After addressing your three examples, let me share something I ran into myself. A few years ago on a business trip to Jinan, I took a taxi back to my hotel after work. Once I got in, communicating with the driver was really difficult. He seemed to understand what I was saying, but I couldn't understand a word he said. Going back to your question: the driver certainly wasn't doing it on purpose—his Mandarin just wasn't standard. Similarly, for the service workers you encountered in Shanghai, age plays the same role, so I don't think there's anything wrong with them speaking Shanghainese first.

Next, to answer your multiple-choice question: during Chinese New Year, a relative visited us with their kid who's in middle school. The kid can understand Shanghainese, but can't speak it at all. Personally, I find that pretty sad. A dialect is also a form of culture, and in another generation or two, it might disappear altogether.
Speaking for myself, I was taught in Mandarin growing up and spoke Mandarin at school. I only spoke Shanghainese at home with my parents. Switching between the two has become an instant, unconscious habit. It's just like an experienced driver: the whole sequence of actions after getting into the car is pure muscle memory, not something they deliberately stop and think through.
So for the question you posed, I'd say the answer is B—not because people forget how to speak Mandarin, but because for someone from Shanghai, blurting out Shanghainese is simply what comes naturally.

Finally, let me share how a local Shanghainese views people from other parts of China living in Shanghai. I have a friend from Zhejiang who has been in Shanghai for maybe five or six years. When driving, he often listens to local radio shows where the hosts frequently speak Shanghainese. For a while, one show even taught a Shanghainese phrase every day, and my friend would mimic it and try to learn. Even though he struggled to pronounce it well, what I saw was someone who wanted to build a life in Shanghai, blend into the city, and learn its culture and language.

I'm sharing this with you in hopes that you'll see it's not Shanghai locals excluding you—it's you who is shutting the city out.
Oh, and one more thing: making sweeping generalizations based on a few encounters and turning them into regional stereotyping is really naive.
